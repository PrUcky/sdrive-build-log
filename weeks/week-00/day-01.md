# Day 01 — The Machine and the Network

**Week:** 00 · **Date:** 2026-08-19 · **Hours:** ~5.5

I started much later in the afternoon than I intended today. I lost the first forty-five minutes literally tearing through boxes and piles of unopened mail in my hallway trying to locate the package containing the Radxa board that got delivered late last week. Once I finally found it wedged between a couple of oversized work envelopes, I cleared off my entire desk, pushed my laptop back against the monitor stand, and laid everything out on a clean cutting mat: the bare green PCB, the small black aluminum heatsink, the SanDisk high-endurance microSD card, and the standalone USB-C power supply I ordered specifically for this build.

## What I actually did

My first reaction when picking up the Radxa ROCK 3C was how unbelievably compact it is in the palm of your hand. You see the dimensions on the spec sheet—85mm by 56mm, the standard Raspberry Pi form factor—but holding it physically makes the whole concept of replacing an entire cloud infrastructure feel almost absurd. 

I spent a good thirty minutes just examining the board topology under a desk lamp before even thinking about plugging anything in. Right in the center sits the Rockchip RK3566 SoC, a modest quad-core ARM Cortex-A55 cluster clocked at up to 1.8GHz. Sitting directly beside it is the LPDDR4 memory chip, followed by the Realtek RTL8211F Gigabit Ethernet transceiver next to the RJ45 jack. Along the perimeter, there is the USB stack (a mix of USB 3.0 and USB 2.0 host ports), the single USB-C port dedicated for power delivery, a micro-HDMI port, the 40-pin GPIO header, and the recovery/maskrom buttons. When I flipped the board over, I spotted the M.2 M-key connector soldered to the underside, right next to the spring-loaded microSD slot.

I deliberately did not try to flash an image or boot the board today. Day 1 was meant to be purely about grounding my mental model and understanding what I am actually building before I start throwing random software at the hardware. So I set the board on an anti-static bag and spent the bulk of the afternoon reading through networking fundamentals and cloud architecture notes.

The biggest mental shift came from deconstructing what "the cloud" actually represents in concrete physical terms. When we talk about Google Photos, we treat it as this abstract, magical entity in the ether that absorbs gigabytes of family memories and indexes them instantaneously. But stripped of marketing, Google Photos is simply thousands of Linux servers running inside climate-controlled warehouses, mounting massive distributed filesystems, writing metadata rows into Spanner or Bigtable, and chunking image payloads into distributed object stores. Alongside that, they run background worker processes scanning every pixel to train machine learning models and serve contextual advertising.

`sdrive` is fundamentally the exact same pipeline stripped of the multi-tenant bloat, the telemetry, and the surveillance business model. It is a single, self-contained appliance sitting quietly on a shelf in my living room. The mobile phone is the client that captures images and requests backup; the ROCK 3C is the server that acknowledges the request and commits the payload to local flash memory. The crucial difference is that in `sdrive`, the server is mathematically blind—it cannot read a single byte of the photo because the encryption happens entirely on the handset before any packet touches the Wi-Fi radio.

That framing made the client/server split click instantly. But it also brought me face-to-face with the first major networking constraint: Network Address Translation (NAT). My home router assigns the board a private RFC 1918 address (like `192.168.1.150`). When my phone is connected to my living room Wi-Fi, reaching the board is trivial. But the moment I step outside, switch to 5G cellular, or connect to a coffee shop network, the board becomes completely invisible behind the router’s firewall and the ISP's Carrier-Grade NAT (CGNAT). I cannot simply forward port 443 without exposing my home network to automated port scanners, and even if I wanted to, CGNAT makes traditional port forwarding impossible because my router does not have a dedicated public IPv4 address. That is the exact problem a peer-to-peer WireGuard mesh like Tailscale is designed to solve. I didn't write any network configs today, but I flagged it as a core topic for Week 02 and Week 06.

I ended the session by grabbing a notebook and drawing out the entire packet path by hand: Phone Camera Sensor -> On-Device Encryption Engine -> Local OS Network Stack -> Cellular Modem -> Cell Tower -> ISP Backbone -> Home Gateway -> Local Ethernet Switch -> Radxa Ethernet PHY -> Linux Kernel Socket -> Storage Daemon. Then I turned the page, hid the first drawing, and drew it again from memory. The second version had fewer decorative arrows and much clearer network boundaries.

## What confused me

The first stumbling block was the power delivery specification. I had naively assumed that any standard 65W USB-C laptop charger I had lying around my office would power the ROCK 3C without issue. But after digging through hardware forum threads, I discovered that single-board computers and smart USB-PD chargers frequently have subtle negotiation incompatibilities. 

If the board lacks a dedicated USB-PD controller IC (like a FUSB302) to actively negotiate 9V or 12V profiles, a modern high-wattage smart charger will default to delivering standard 5V, but it might not handle rapid transient current spikes gracefully. When a quad-core ARM processor suddenly bursts from idle to 100% utilization while simultaneously powering an active Gigabit Ethernet transceiver and writing to storage, the voltage can momentarily dip below 4.75V. That voltage drop triggers an immediate kernel panic or silent hardware reboot known as a brownout. Seeing so many developers pulling their hair out over phantom SD card corruption caused entirely by noisy power supplies convinced me to stick strictly to a dedicated, high-quality 5V/3A regulated power brick.

The second thing that gave me pause was the M.2 slot on the underside of the board. The documentation was slightly ambiguous on whether the slot provided a native PCIe lane or if it was muxed with SATA/USB 3.0. After cross-referencing the Radxa wiki schematics, I confirmed it exposes a single PCIe 2.1 lane (offering a theoretical bandwidth limit of roughly 500 MB/s). That is more than four times faster than a Gigabit Ethernet link (which caps out at 125 MB/s), meaning that when I eventually add an NVMe SSD in the production revision, the storage bus will never be the bottleneck during network ingestion.
